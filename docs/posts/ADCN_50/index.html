Sports Science Research Needs Its Own Tech Stack

The first time I heard the term “technology stack” was during the build-out stage of the Internet. There was LAMP: Linux, Apache, MySQL, and PHP/Python. It was (sometimes still is) how to put a secure, reliable, interactive service on the World Wide Web. The term “stack” lives on, and even though it varies from one task to another, it still refers to the list of essential software elements needed for an Internet-based service.

O’Reilly Publishing championed LAMP and now touts PARK, an AI stack for model builders that uses PyTorch, AI, Ray, and Kubernetes.

Sports science has a technology stack. Versions and references to it are easy to find: Svexa, Justin Ochoa, Vinciamo Sports, Driveline Baseball, FYTT. The stack’s service revolves around collecting, analyzing, and reporting data related to athletes’ health and performance. This is a lot of what I research, and I have lots to say, but first a story.

During an earlier stint in grad school, I interned with a big architecture firm that designed biomedical research labs. I wanted to learn how to build technology into collaborative research environments.

The architects I worked with spent long hours in conversation with lab directors, asking them what they wanted in their facility. These directors were the actual building designers. The process usually worked. I remember one time that it didn’t.

The client needed a biocontainment laboratory and decided to put it on an upper floor in a new building for security. It also needed to bring cattle-size animals to their biosafety-level 4 rooms, requiring a giant elevator close to the loading dock. The architect asked the scientist, “How should we make this work for you?” The scientist told the architect, “I don’t know. You tell me. You’re the expert.” But the architect wasn’t an expert, just an assembler. Fortunately, the architect had a boss who was expert and had an answer.

Expertise is valuable in order to do things that haven’t been done (or tried) before. The technology stack for sports science is suboptimal for doing sports science research. Mollie Brewer just published a paper for a conference in Germany saying as much.

Sports science research has its version of the cow-elevator question: What goes into a sports science technology stack in order to meet the needs of researchers? The answer begs for an expert, and I’d like to go, “two thumbs pointing right here,” but at present all I can do is outline the problem and describe a path to its solution. It’s something less than a solution. Sorry. I’m working on it.

One complication for this particular tech stack is the range of different users who share tasks related to athletes’ data. Performance coaches, medical staff, analysts, and sports administrators rely on some version of the athletes’ data stack for decision-making. For many of these athletes’ data stakeholders with admin responsibility, the tech stack is a single-vendor, turnkey athlete management system, and it’s not designed for research.

A growing consensus among AMS users has formed around the information overload issue, where too much data in their systems has slowed decision processes and created bottlenecks. “I’d rather build something that helps athletes, coaches, clinicians, and leaders understand together what’s happening, be honest about what they can’t see, act, and learn from it,” Bryce Murphy from IMG writes in a recent Teamworks blog post.

Streamlining can be difficult. Vendor systems gain customers and improve lock-in by adding functionality. Alternative custom tech stacks assembled LAMP-style from Open Source components can be a Frankenstein that requires substantial technical and engineering know-how. The University of Waterloo has a student-based sports analytics support group that published its tech stack at JSM in August.

The Waterloo tech stack supports 10-20 analysts and 8 intercollegiate teams. The infrastructure allows analysts to work in different programming languages (R, Python) and to blend personal and group workflows (unrestricted IDEs, GitHub, internal support packages). Conventions for code style and filenames keep things organized. Reports are produced in Quarto (static) and Shiny (interactive), chosen for their cross-language support, and are distributed via Posit Connect Cloud (easy automation, shareable URLs).

Brewer’s paper describes user interactions with the UF Athletics Databank, as created by the AI-Powered Athletics initiative at the University of Florida. According to the university website, “The databank creates a scalable foundation for advanced analytics and artificial intelligence research in real-world collegiate sport settings, while also supporting informed decision-making and athlete performance operations across Gator Athletics.”

The care and effort to produce a shared data resource like this that is both secure and accessible is necessary for a research-focused athletes’ data tech stack. By itself the databank is one part of what’s needed. A university environment can assume high levels of technical expertise and also assume that users expect the freedom to create their own tech stack geared to the type of research under consideration. But the wheel sometimes gets reinvented over and over.

What is needed to prevent the duplication is an open, interoperable layer in the tech stack that fits in between the data and the end-service. This is something that the JupyterHealth project seeks to provide research-based healthcare developers. The project recently earned a $10 million moonshot grant from the Laude Institute to build out its platform.

JupyterHealth builds on the Project Jupyter, which created the ubiquitous notebook interface for coding in Python. Jupyter notebooks evolved hand-in-hand with the emergence of pervasive data science in academia and elsewhere. (FYI: the Athletes Data Community Newsletter has a precursor, the Data Science Community Newsletter, which ran from 2015-25 and peaked at 10,000 readers.)

Jupyter notebooks bundle and assemble data and code. JupyterHealth adds privacy-preserving health data security that enable personal and public health services to scale up, first to the community level but realistically, eventually to population levels. This is health developer infrastructure.

According to a recent editorial by Avinash Chandran and Nicol van Dyk in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, infrastructure improvements are what’s needed to unlock piles of accumulated athletes’ data and mitigate sports injuries at a global scale.

Applying JupyterHealth to research questions in sports offers a collaborative framework and infrastructure for all sorts of grand challenges, like the computational epidemiology proposed by Chandran and van Dyk. Consider what’s going on in Sports Human-Computer Interaction, Health Visualization, Wearable Technologies, and OpenSim biomechanics.

A platform like JupyterHealth that coalesces like-minded researchers on large problems can also connect health practitioners to individuals with the same sort of workflow. Bundle and assemble data and analysis, then use it to create services, but take it all the way to the edge to ingest, process, and ultimately, feedback individual health information. Ida Sim, a JupyterHealth director and UCSF primary care physician, puts it this way: “What if the most important person on your care team was you?”

Collaborative work is possible with commercial athlete management systems. Teamworks has a product that the Southeastern Conference uses to share athlete health records across universities. Catherine O’Neal, the person in charge of the SEC medical program, explains the use case, “We love to buy 15 different programs and then hope that some person who’s really supposed to be taking care of athletes at a bedside can go in the middle of the night and put all that data together, usually in an Excel spreadsheet.” Useful, but more operational than research.

Nowhere is that more evident than when it comes to AI. The Athletic describes how the University of Florida uses its athletic databank with AI to make specific data available to trainers so that they can put it to use. Other schools (University of Nebraska, for one) use it as a game strategy assistant. The first example is pedestrian. The second is risky. Neither is especially insightful.

Teamworks CTO Reed Shaffner outlined the role AI should play in smart sports organizations. It is “a specialized layer sitting between your organization and whatever frontier models exist, with the models plugging in underneath as interchangeable engines. If one gets taken away tomorrow, your intelligence remains, because it was never stored in the model. It was stored in the layer you own.”

JupyterHealth can function as a control and verification layer for AI model effectiveness in a high-privacy sandbox. The expertise that goes into bundling and assembling data and analysis should come with sufficient understanding of what AI can accurately contribute to an original research question.

The stakes are higher for researchers when it comes to that specialized layer, and it will also be important for sports organizations that seek competitive advantage from research with data, and not just evidence-based decision-making.
